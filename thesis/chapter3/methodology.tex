\section{Introduction}
This chapter describes the proposed data pipeline, volatility models, anomaly labels,
detectors and evaluation protocol. The study is quantitative and experimental. Two
comparisons drive it: a detector trained on raw returns against the same detector trained
on GARCH-standardized returns, and a detector trained on global market indices and applied
to the Dhaka Stock Exchange (DSE) against one trained on DSE data. So far the DSE data
sources have been identified and the first volatility model run (Chapter~4) has been
carried out; the DSE download has not yet been run. The rest of the chapter describes the
proposed design.

\section{Research Design}
The study follows a deductive, experimental design: each hypothesis in Chapter~1 is
tested by a controlled comparison in which one factor changes and everything else is held
fixed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1} is tested by training the same autoencoder, with the same
    architecture, hyperparameters, data and seeds, on raw returns and on GARCH-standardized
    returns, and comparing event-based F1 and false alarms by volatility regime.
    \item \textbf{RQ2} is tested by training detectors on global indices and evaluating them
    on the DSE test block without retraining, after fine-tuning, and against detectors
    trained on DSE data only.
    \item \textbf{RQ3} is tested by scoring every detector, including the baselines, with
    event-based F1, point-adjusted F1 and PA\%K on the same predictions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Collection}

\subsection{Global Market Data}
Global index data come from Yahoo Finance through the \texttt{yfinance} Python package. The
first index used is the S\&P~500 (\texttt{\^{}GSPC}), daily, from January 2010 to December
2025. Further developed-market indices will be added as transfer sources. The final
list will be fixed together with the labelling rules, before any detector is trained.

The preliminary results in Chapter~4 were produced before Yahoo Finance could be reached
from the development environment. They use the copy of S\&P~500 daily prices that ships with
the Python \texttt{arch} package, which covers January 1999 to December 2018 and was
originally sourced from Yahoo Finance.

\subsection{Dhaka Stock Exchange Data}\label{sec:dse-data}
The DSE publishes daily data for listed stocks in its day-end archive, with open, high,
low and close prices, the previous close (\texttt{ycp}), the number of trades, traded value
and volume. For long-listed stocks the archive goes back to about 1999. Index levels come
from the DSE market summary pages. Both sources are accessed through the open-source
\texttt{bdshare} package. A public dataset on Mendeley (DSE historical data, 1999 to April
2025, over 700 tickers) is used to cross-check the pull. It has no previous-close field, so
it cannot be used to detect corporate-action adjustments.

The market index is the DSE Broad Index (DSEX), which starts on 28 January 2013. Before
2013 the exchange published the DSE General Index (DGEN), which used a different method. The
two series are not spliced, so the DSE index sample begins in 2013.

Intraday DSE data are not freely available; the only historical source found is a paid
commercial feed. This thesis therefore uses daily data only.

The DSE sources have been identified and the download and quality-check scripts
written, but the full pull has not yet been run, because the DSE website was not reachable
from the development environment. The pull will be run from a team member's machine. The
study proceeds with DSE daily data if DSEX has at least 3{,}000 rows from 2013 and at least
ten stocks have ten or more years of data with under 2\% missing days outside floor-price
periods. Otherwise the target market changes to India, using the NIFTY~50 index and
large-cap NSE stocks from \texttt{yfinance} (Section~\ref{sec:risks}).

\section{Data Cleaning and Preprocessing}
Returns are daily log returns in percent, $r_t = 100\ln(P_t/P_{t-1})$. Scaling by 100 keeps
the GARCH optimiser well conditioned.

DSE prices need four corrections before returns are computed.

\textbf{Floor prices.} The Bangladesh Securities and Exchange Commission (BSEC) imposed
minimum prices from about March 2020 to June 2021 and again from July 2022 to January 2024,
with partial lifting in December 2022 and reimposition in March 2023. During these periods
many stocks printed the same close with near-zero volume for months. Such flat runs are not
calm markets and must not be treated as normal behaviour. They are flagged as a separate
regime, and results are reported with and without them. The dates above come from press
reports at month level and will be checked against BSEC orders before the label rules are
fixed.

\textbf{Bonus shares and other corporate actions.} DSE prices are not adjusted, and
Bangladeshi firms issue bonus shares often, so raw closes fall sharply on record dates. The
DSE resets the previous close after a corporate action. A day on which \texttt{ycp} differs
from the prior day's close is treated as an adjustment day, and the earlier part of the
series is scaled by the ratio of the two. This rule is an inference about how the DSE sets
\texttt{ycp} and will be spot-checked against known bonus dates.

\textbf{Price limits.} Daily moves on the DSE are capped, at about $\pm 10\%$ for most of
the sample, so returns are truncated. Days that close at a limit are flagged and are not
treated as ordinary extreme returns.

\textbf{Calendar and liquidity.} DSEX trading dates define the calendar. Missing days and
zero-volume days are counted for each stock, and the final 10 to 20 stocks are chosen by
median daily turnover.

\section{Train, Validation and Test Splits}
All splits are by date, with no shuffling, so that no information from a later period is
used to fit or tune anything evaluated on an earlier one. For the S\&P~500 from
\texttt{yfinance}, the training block ends on 31 December 2018, validation covers 2019 to
2021, and the test block is everything from 2022 on. For the preliminary run on the bundled
1999 to 2018 series, training covers 1999 to 2012, validation 2013 to 2015 and test 2016 to
2018. DSE split dates will be fixed after the pull, so that each block contains both
calm and volatile periods and the floor-price periods can be excluded or reported
separately without emptying any block. The dates are written into the configuration file
before any detector is trained.

\section{Volatility Modelling and Standardization}
Each return is modelled as a constant mean plus an innovation, $r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t$,
with $\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t e_t$ and $e_t$ drawn from a Student-$t$ distribution with
$\nu$ degrees of freedom, scaled to unit variance. Three models for the conditional variance
are compared. GARCH(1,1)~\cite{bollerslev1986generalized}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2 .
\end{equation}
GJR-GARCH(1,1,1)~\cite{glosten1993relation}, where $I_{t-1}=1$ if $\varepsilon_{t-1}<0$ and
$0$ otherwise:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \left(\alpha + \gamma I_{t-1}\right)\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2 .
\end{equation}
EGARCH(1,1,1)~\cite{nelson1991conditional}:
\begin{equation}
\ln\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\left(|e_{t-1}| - \sqrt{2/\pi}\right) + \gamma\, e_{t-1} + \beta\ln\sigma_{t-1}^2 .
\end{equation}
In GJR a positive $\gamma$, and in EGARCH a negative $\gamma$, means that negative returns
raise volatility more than positive returns of the same size.

Parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood on the training block only. The fitted
parameters are then held fixed and the model is run forward over the whole series as a
one-step-ahead filter, so $\sigma_t$ on any validation or test day depends only on returns
up to day $t-1$ and on parameters estimated from training data. The standardized residual
is $z_t = (r_t - \mu)/\sigma_t$. All models are fitted with the \texttt{arch} Python package.

Model adequacy is checked on each block separately with Ljung-Box tests~\cite{ljung1978measure}
on $z_t$ and $z_t^2$ at 10 and 20 lags, an ARCH-LM test at 10 lags, and a Jarque-Bera test
of normality. The volatility model used for labels and inputs is chosen on the validation
diagnostics, not on training-sample BIC alone, because the preliminary results in
Chapter~4 show that the two can disagree. Before labels are fixed, the frozen-parameter
filter will be compared with rolling or expanding re-estimation.

\section{Anomaly Label Construction}

\subsection{Statistical Labels from Standardized Residuals}
A day is labelled anomalous if its standardized residual falls in the two-sided tail of
probability $p$ under the fitted unit-variance Student-$t$ distribution:
\begin{equation}
|z_t| > c, \qquad c = t_{\nu,\,1-p/2}\sqrt{\frac{\nu-2}{\nu}} ,
\end{equation}
where $t_{\nu,q}$ is the $q$ quantile of the standard Student-$t$ distribution. With
$p = 0.002$, a correctly specified model flags about one day in 500.

For comparison, a naive rule without a volatility model standardizes returns by the
training-block mean and standard deviation and flags days beyond the normal quantile for
the same tail probability.

\subsection{Known Market Events}
Days with a documented market event are labelled from public records: market-wide crashes,
regulatory actions such as BSEC orders that introduce or lift floor prices, and major
national or global news. Each event is entered with its date and a source. An event can
span several days, in which case it is labelled as one segment. The event list is compiled
from the records alone, without looking at detector output.

\subsection{Injected Anomalies}
For controlled tests, synthetic anomalies are inserted into copies of the test series:
single-day spikes, short level shifts and short bursts of raised volatility. Magnitudes are
expressed as multiples of the local conditional standard deviation, so an injected anomaly
is equally hard to find in calm and volatile periods. Injection uses its own random seed
(the base seed plus 1000), so changing a model's seed never changes the injected test set.
Injected anomalies are used only to test detectors, never to train them.

\subsection{Fixing the Rules in Advance}
All three label rules, including thresholds, event sources and injection settings, are
written in \texttt{docs/labeling\_rules.md} and fixed before any detector is trained. They
are not changed after results are seen. Results are reported separately for each label
source, because each measures something different: market significance, statistical
surprise and controlled detectability.

\section{Detection Models}

\subsection{Baselines}
Four baselines are compared with the proposed model: a threshold on the raw-return z-score;
a threshold on the GARCH-standardized residual, which is the statistical label rule used as
a detector; an isolation forest on windows of returns; and a plain LSTM autoencoder on raw
returns. Including simple baselines matters because large evaluations of time-series anomaly
detectors find that simple methods are often competitive~\cite{schmidl2022anomaly}.

\subsection{Autoencoder on Raw versus GARCH-Standardized Returns}
The main model is an LSTM encoder-decoder autoencoder~\cite{malhotra2016lstm}. It reads a
sliding window of $w$ consecutive daily inputs, compresses it into a fixed-length vector and
reconstructs the window. It is trained on the training block only, so it learns
reconstruction of mostly normal behaviour. The anomaly score $s_t$ of day $t$ is the
reconstruction error of that day in the window that ends on it, and a day is flagged when
$s_t > \tau$, with the threshold $\tau$ set as a quantile of the validation-block scores.

Two versions are trained. The first takes raw log returns, scaled by the training-block mean
and standard deviation. The second takes the GARCH-standardized residuals $z_t$. Architecture,
window length, hyperparameters, training data and seeds are identical, so any difference in
results comes from the input. The window length and network size are chosen on the
validation block of the raw-return model and then reused unchanged for the standardized one.
A Transformer-based variant~\cite{xu2022anomaly} will be added only if time allows.

\section{Transfer Learning Protocol}
The source domain is the set of global market indices and the target domain is the DSE.
Detectors are trained on the pooled training blocks of the global indices. They are then
evaluated on the DSE test block in three settings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zero-shot:} the trained detector is applied to DSE inputs without any
    change to its weights.
    \item \textbf{Fine-tuned:} training continues on the DSE training block, with early
    stopping on the DSE validation block.
    \item \textbf{DSE only:} the same detector is trained from scratch on DSE data, as a
    reference.
\end{enumerate}

The volatility model is always estimated on the DSE training block for DSE inputs, because
GARCH parameters describe one market's volatility and are not meant to transfer. The
threshold $\tau$ is set on the DSE validation block in all three settings. Both the raw and
the standardized autoencoder go through the same protocol, which also shows whether
standardization makes inputs from different markets more alike and so eases transfer.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
Detectors are scored against labelled anomalous events. Precision, recall and $F_1$ are
\begin{equation}
Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \qquad Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
F_1=\frac{2\times Precision \times Recall}{Precision+Recall},
\end{equation}
where $TP$, $FP$ and $FN$ count true positives, false positives and false negatives.

Three versions of $F_1$ are reported together. Event-based $F_1$ counts each labelled event
once. Point-adjusted $F_1$ (F1-PA)~\cite{xu2018unsupervised} counts every point of an event as
detected if any one of its points is detected. PA\%K~\cite{kim2022towards} applies the
adjustment only when at least $K$\% of the event's points are detected; it is reported
for values of $K$ from 0 to 100. Because point adjustment can make random scores look
competitive~\cite{kim2022towards}, the gap between F1-PA and the stricter scores is
reported as a result in its own right.

\section{Multiple Seeds and Regime Analysis}
Every deep model is trained with five fixed seeds (0 to 4), chosen before any results, and
results are reported as mean and standard deviation over all five. No seed is dropped after
its result is seen.

To test whether a detector flags turbulent periods rather than unusual days, test days are
divided into calm and volatile regimes by their conditional volatility, with the cut-off set
from the training block. False alarms and recall are then reported separately for each
regime. For the DSE, floor-price periods form a third regime.

\section{Leakage Controls}
The design prevents information from the evaluation period reaching any fitted component.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Data are split by date only, never at random.
    \item Volatility models, scalers, detectors and thresholds are fitted on training or
    validation data only. Later volatility estimates use rolling or expanding re-fits that
    see only past data.
    \item Labelling rules are fixed before any detector is trained.
    \item Hyperparameters are chosen on validation data, and the test block is used once
    per final configuration.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility}
All settings, including split dates, model specifications and label thresholds, are kept in
one configuration file (\texttt{config.yaml}) in the project repository. Random seeds are
fixed at 42 for Python, NumPy and PyTorch. The code is public at
\url{https://github.com/smhasibul0/dse-anomaly-detection}, and package versions are pinned
in its \texttt{requirements.txt}. Every result is logged with its seed and configuration.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
The study uses public market prices only and no personal data. The DSE website terms and
the licence of the Mendeley dataset have not been reviewed, so the code and download
scripts will be released but raw DSE prices will not be redistributed until the terms are
confirmed.

\section{Limitations of the Methodology}
The data are daily, so anomalies that begin and end within a trading day are invisible.
DSE prices are distorted by floor-price regimes and price limits, and the corporate-action
adjustment relies on an inferred rule. Statistical labels depend on the chosen volatility
model and threshold, and real manipulation cases on the DSE are not publicly labelled, so
the study cannot claim to detect manipulation.

\section{Summary of the Chapter}
The proposed method builds a leakage-safe pipeline for global and DSE daily data, removes
the volatility level from returns with GARCH-family models fitted on training data, labels
anomalies from three sources fixed in advance, and compares an autoencoder on raw and on
standardized returns, within and across markets. Evaluation uses event-based and
point-adjusted scores over five seeds, with results broken down by volatility regime.
